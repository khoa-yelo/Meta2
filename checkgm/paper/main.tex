\title{checkGM: A Reference-Based Framework\\for Interpreting the Gut Microbiome}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Khoa Hoang}\IEEEauthorblockA{Stanford University, Stanford, CA, USA}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Genomics has a reference genome and clinical chemistry has a normal range, but microbiome analysis usually stops at quantification: hundreds of abundances are reported with nothing to judge any one of them against. checkGM closes that gap by distributing two standard analysis pipelines together with reference baselines built from public cohorts processed identically, so that every taxon, gene family and pathway of a new sample is returned as a percentile of the reference population with a call of within, below or above the reference range. MGnify v5 is reproduced by the assembly pipeline (1{,}941 reference adults, 26 studies) and curatedMetagenomicData~3 by the read pipeline (6{,}494 adults, 19 studies); each baseline travels as summary statistics, with no sample-level data. What a reference range yields on its own is a single number, the share of a sample's features falling outside it, and on fourteen held-out case/control studies that number barely separates cases from controls: a mean AUROC of 0.564, below the 0.615 of the Gut Microbiome Health Index. The same deviations read feature by feature reach 0.720, higher in 13 of the 14 studies. Deviation from a reference is therefore informative per feature and not in aggregate, which is the case for reporting every feature rather than a score. Read as calibration rather than contest, that per-feature score is level with GMWI2 (0.725) on studies that index was partly trained on and ahead of it on the four neither has seen (0.634 against 0.608), using half the features the same model needs on raw abundances. The ranges are approximately calibrated, reference adults falling outside them at a median of 4.9 and 7.1\% of families per pipeline against the 5\% expected by construction. What the reference adds beyond a score is interpretation: the loss of obligate anaerobes is read off individual \cdiff{} patients, the depletion of fibre-fermenting commensals is recovered across nine colorectal cancer cohorts on one common scale, and the taxa that place Hadza hunter-gatherers outside an industrialized range are named rather than merely scored. Asked of all 17 countries the read baseline spans, that caution holds for two of them at every taxonomic rank and for two more at species level alone, while most of the variation between populations cannot be told apart from the variation between studies --- so a reference range transfers unevenly, and the unevenness is measurable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Genomics interprets a variant against a reference genome, and clinical chemistry interprets a measurement against a normal range, the spread of values observed in healthy people measured on the same assay~\cite{horn2003}. Microbiome analysis has neither. Hundreds of taxa, gene families and pathways are quantified by current profilers~\cite{beghini2021,richardson2023}, yet whether any value among them is unusual is decided separately by each study, against whichever controls it happened to recruit. Quantification is where the analysis stops. An isolated profile therefore cannot be interpreted at all, since the comparison group it would require is absent, and effect sizes obtained in different studies cannot be placed on a common axis, a long-recognised obstacle to meta-analysis~\cite{duvallet2017}.

The usual workaround condenses a profile into a single health index. GMHI~\cite{gupta2020} and its successor GMWI2~\cite{chang2024} do this well enough to be useful, and both buy comparability at the same price: the resulting number reports that a sample looks diseased without saying which of its features are unusual, or by how much. A clinician handed such a score cannot act on it, because nothing in it indicates what to investigate; and because the index is a fitted function of the profile, its value cannot be checked against anything a reader can inspect. Nor can the gap be blamed on a shortage of reference data, since tens of thousands of gut metagenomes carrying a healthy or control label have been deposited~\cite{richardson2023,antonello2025}. It is explained instead by two properties of the data that exist. A range is valid only for measurements produced the same way, because profiles from different pipelines are not interchangeable~\cite{sczyrba2017}, so a range and the software computing it must be distributed as one object; and public cohorts are released as per-sample tables that must be re-processed before anything new can be compared against them. What has been missing is consequently not another reference cohort but a pipeline carrying its own baseline.

checkGM is proposed here to supply exactly that (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). Two prebuilt baselines are shipped with the pipelines that produced them: a reimplementation of the MGnify v5 assembly workflow~\cite{richardson2023}, and a read-based pipeline reproducing curatedMetagenomicData~3 (cMD3)~\cite{pasolli2017}. Each baseline is drawn from the resource its pipeline reproduces and summarized across the studies and samples of Table~\ref{tab:cohorts} --- 1{,}941 adults from 26 studies and 6{,}494 from 19 respectively, reference in the sense a clinical reference interval uses, adults carrying a healthy or control label in the source metadata rather than adults whose health was verified here. Once a sample is supplied, every feature is returned with its percentile in that reference population and a call of within, below or above the range, together with the features most reference adults carry but this sample lacks. From the same output a \emph{checkGM deviation score} is derived, a sparse supervised model over a sample's reference percentiles that quantifies how far the sample as a whole departs from the reference in the direction that distinguishes disease.

Taken together, four claims are made for the framework and tested in turn below. Its output is interpretable, every number being a percentile of a published baseline rather than a fitted quantity. Deviation from the reference is informative per feature and not in aggregate: the single number a reference range yields on its own is beaten even by GMHI, while a sparse model over the same percentiles matches a state-of-the-art index without that index's overlap with the test studies. Known disease associations are recovered from the ranges, both within a single cohort and across nine. And shifts from the reference are indicated per feature, which turns an unexplained score into a list of candidates for follow-up --- and, in the one population here that the baselines do not fit, shows exactly where the method's own limits lie.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}

Both baselines are curated from public resources rather than from newly collected material, which is what allows the cohorts behind them to be large and the summaries to be redistributable. For the assembly pipeline, 19{,}437 MGnify v5 analyses were inventoried from 130 studies; 17{,}780 were of human metagenomes, and 9{,}195 gut metagenomes were retained from those, one analysis per sample across 69 studies. Of these, 8{,}742 passed assembly quality filters of at least 5~Mb assembled and 20 of 52 single-copy marker genes detected. For the read pipeline, 9{,}246 cMD3 MetaPhlAn~3 profiles from 37 studies were taken with their curated metadata~\cite{antonello2025}, and 7{,}478 adult stool controls were retained, each with a profile at least 90\% resolvable to an NCBI family. Health status, age and sex were obtained from a manual curation of the study and sample metadata in both cases. Table~\ref{tab:cohorts} gives the counts at each step.

\subsection{Pipelines}

So that a new sample can be placed on exactly the scale its baseline was built on, each source measurement is reproduced from raw reads inside a pinned container (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a).

\emph{Assembly pipeline.} The MGnify v5 workflow is reproduced step for step. Assembly is performed by metaSPAdes 3.15.3~\cite{nurk2017} or MEGAHIT 1.2.9~\cite{li2015}, genes are called by Prodigal 2.6.3~\cite{hyatt2010} and FragGeneScan~\cite{rho2010}, taxonomy is assigned by DIAMOND~\cite{buchfink2015} against UniRef90, and function by eggNOG-mapper~\cite{huerta2017}, KOfam~\cite{aramaki2020} and InterProScan~\cite{jones2014}. Taxa are expressed as coverage-weighted proportions of assigned sequence, and genes as copies per genome scaled by the coverage of the marker genes~\cite{nayfach2015}. Taxonomy resolves to genus, and the functional layers are KEGG orthologs, Pfam domains and KEGG modules.

\emph{Read pipeline.} Profiling is performed by MetaPhlAn 3.0.14~\cite{beghini2021} with the mpa\_v30 markers on untrimmed reads, as in cMD3, and HUMAnN~3 gene-family and pathway tables may be added, grouped into KEGG orthologs (KOs) and MetaCyc pathways~\cite{caspi2020}. Taxonomy resolves to species. MetaPhlAn~3 is pinned rather than the later MetaPhlAn~4~\cite{blanco2023} because the baseline must be measured the way the resource it reproduces was measured; a baseline on the newer marker set would be a different object, not an improved one, and could not be compared against cMD3 percentiles.

\subsection{Reference range establishment}

The two baselines are built by one procedure, differing at a single step. Compositional layers are transformed to centred log-ratios~\cite{aitchison1982} over the features that clear a 5\% prevalence threshold, applied to the baseline samples in the read pipeline but to all 8{,}742 quality-passing gut analyses in the assembly pipeline, so that one feature space is shared there by both inclusion tiers and by every leave-one-study-out rebuild. The resulting assembly basis holds 584 families and 1{,}687 genera, every one of which also clears the threshold among the 1{,}941 baseline samples by themselves, so the wider population decides which features exist but not which of them the baseline would have kept. Every stored statistic is then computed from the baseline samples only. For each surviving feature, its prevalence within each size class---fixed thresholds on assembled length in the assembly pipeline, at 5--90~Mb, 90--180~Mb and above, and tertiles of read depth in the read pipeline---and its 1st--99th percentiles among the samples that carry it are stored, with studies weighted equally so that the largest cohorts cannot dominate the range. Percentiles are given only where at least 20 detections support them, and for the assembly pipeline's four gene and module layers they are stored within each size class as well; the taxonomic layers are not stratified that way.

One inclusion rule admits a sample to a baseline: a healthy or control label in the source metadata, with any sample labelled diseased or under treatment excluded. The rule is deliberately the weaker of the two that the metadata would support, since requiring the label to be present rather than merely uncontradicted, and requiring the recorded age, body-mass index, pregnancy and antibiotic fields to pass as well, costs a fifth of the assembly baseline for a population no better characterised; what that definition cannot deliver is taken up in the limitations rather than filtered for here.

Scoring inverts those same rules. A new sample is normalized exactly as the baseline samples were, and its percentile for each feature is obtained by interpolation between the stored quantiles, those of the sample's own size class taking precedence wherever the bundle holds them. In the assembly pipeline a gene or module feature is assessed only where it reaches 0.05 copies per genome; below that it is left unassessed rather than called, since the coverage behind it is too thin to place. A feature is then called \textbf{low} or \textbf{high} when it falls outside the 2.5th--97.5th percentile range, and \textbf{expected but missing} when it is carried by at least 70\% of reference samples of its size class but not by the sample at hand. The share of a sample's features lying outside the range, its \emph{fraction outside}, should be about 5\% when the sample belongs to the reference population, and it is that expectation which makes the baselines testable. Because only these summaries are kept, never the samples from which they were derived, a baseline can be redistributed where the cohort behind it could not be: the stored statistics occupy 11~MiB for the assembly bundle and 3~MiB for the read bundle, and the deposited archives come to 52 and 62~MiB only because each also ships the NCBI taxonomy backbone that the normalizer requires, and the read bundle HUMAnN's KO-to-UniRef90 map.

\subsection{Evaluation against existing tools and metrics}

Three comparisons are made, each against a method in current use, and all of them under one model class. Every representation is scored by the same L1-penalized logistic regression, so that a difference between two representations cannot be a difference of classifier; a single model class also means that what is compared is the input and not the learner's capacity. Each left-out study is classified by a model trained on all the other case/control studies, with the penalty selected by grouped cross-validation inside the training studies alone. The representations compared are the reference percentiles and the raw centred log-ratios they are computed from, on the same features and the same samples.

GMHI~\cite{gupta2020} and GMWI2~\cite{chang2024} were scored on the read pipeline exactly as published, without retraining. Neither index is met on wholly equal terms. GMHI is defined on species, so on the assembly pipeline, whose taxonomy is resolved to genus, it could only be approximated from the ratio of its health-associated to its health-depleted genera; and even on the read pipeline only 33 of its 50 species are matched by name in MetaPhlAn~3. Both figures should therefore be read as lower bounds on what that index achieves on its native input.

So that those indices would be met on their own ground, the \emph{checkGM deviation score} was pre-specified: an L1-penalized logistic regression on a sample's reference percentiles, its fraction outside, its number of expected-but-missing features and the presence of each species. The score is supervised --- its weights are fitted on case/control labels, not derived from the reference alone --- and what makes it a deviation score is its input, every feature entering as a departure from the reference rather than as an abundance. A percentile is itself that departure, expressed as a rank among reference adults, so each term of the score is a deviation multiplied by a learned weight. Model and features were fixed before any result was seen and each study was scored by a model trained without it. The variant the software distributes, and the variant reported as the score throughout, drops the species-presence features for the reason given in Section~IV-B; it is the only variant plotted, since a figure should not report a model nobody can run.

Paired AUROC comparisons were made by a Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the left-out studies, two-sided throughout, no direction having been fixed in advance for any comparison reported below. Where a difference runs in the expected direction the one-sided value is half the two-sided one.

\subsection{Case study cohorts and analysis}

Three sets were fixed before any sample was scored (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). The baselines themselves hold 1{,}941 reference adults from 26 studies in the assembly pipeline and 6{,}494 from 19 studies in the read pipeline. Three further reference studies per pipeline, 233 and 317 adults, were held out entirely, and are used only to test calibration. The disease studies form the case/control sets: 2{,}294 samples from 11 studies in the assembly pipeline and 1{,}437 from 15 studies in the read pipeline. Of the latter, the 14 studies with at least 20 cases and 20 controls enter the classification benchmark, the fifteenth being small enough that it is used only for the disease-shift analyses. The single-cohort case study is the \cdiff{} infection arm of the assembly pipeline (56 patients, 14 controls); the cross-cohort case study is the nine colorectal cancer cohorts of the read pipeline; and the population case study is the Hadza hunter-gatherers of Tanzania, sequenced alongside Nepali and Californian comparison groups~\cite{carter2023}.

The \emph{shift} of a feature in a case/control study is defined as the median percentile of the cases minus that of the controls of the same study. A shift is called significant when it reaches at least 10 points at $q \leq 0.05$ (Mann--Whitney with Benjamini--Hochberg correction~\cite{benjamini1995}), and \emph{consistent} when it holds in the same direction in at least two studies of one condition.

Everything reported below rests on one condition: no sample was ever compared with a baseline that contained it. Baseline studies were accordingly scored against a baseline rebuilt without them, and the cases and controls of a disease study were always scored against the same baseline, so that a difference between them cannot be an artifact of the reference. In the read pipeline the three sets share neither a sample nor a study. In the assembly pipeline the held-out cohorts are separate in the same way, but the baseline and the case/control set are not: all 11 case/control studies are among the 26 baseline studies, and the 887 controls of those studies are precisely their baseline members, so each of the 887 counts in both sets and is scored, like the cases beside it, against a baseline rebuilt without its study. Specimens deposited twice are harder, and the rule that handles them is a property of the study rather than of the sample: a study sharing specimens with a baseline study is scored against a baseline rebuilt without that study too. Two cases required it. All 30 controls of GuptaA\_2019 are also deposited in DhakanDB\_2019 and none of its 30 cases is, so a per-sample rule would score its controls and its cases against different baselines, aligning the reference with the label; and LeChatelierE\_2013 and NielsenHB\_2014 are two cMD3 deposits of the same Danish cohort, 171 specimens held in the pool twice, so holding out one deposit alone would leave the other deposit's profile of the same specimen in the baseline that scores it. Both now resolve to a single combined hold-out bundle, and no duplicated specimen is placed on both sides of a training and test split. Counting each sample once, the three assembly-pipeline sets hold 3{,}581 of the 8{,}742 samples that pass quality control, drawn from 29 of the 68 studies that survive it; the remaining 5{,}161 enter none of the analyses below.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The reference ranges are calibrated and the pipelines reproduce their sources}

If the stored quantiles describe reference adults, then reference adults a baseline has never seen should fall outside its range about 5\% of the time, and that expectation is met on both pipelines (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). On the three held-out cohorts per pipeline, the most nearly independent test, a median of 4.9\% of families is placed outside the range by the assembly baseline and 7.1\% by the read baseline. The larger, semi-independent test in which each baseline study is scored against a baseline rebuilt without it gives 5.4\% and 5.6\%. Each of those four figures is the median over studies of a study's mean per-sample fraction outside---the one summary both pipelines record; the per-sample distribution is right-skewed, the study mean exceeding the study median in all 93 leave-one-study-out rows of the read pipeline, so a median of medians runs 0.8 to 2.7 points lower on the layers where both are recorded and the two must not be mixed. The held-out test is not wholly independent on the assembly pipeline, whose log-ratio basis was prevalence-filtered over all quality-passing analyses and so counted the 233 held-out adults, which moves 3 of its 584 families and 22 of its 1{,}687 genera; no stored quantile, however, was fitted on a held-out sample. The agreement is also not uniform across layers, since every layer but one falls between 4.7 and 7.2\% around the nominal 5\%, most of them a little above it and the read pipeline's held-out taxonomy highest, at 6.8 to 7.2\%. The exception is the KEGG modules of the assembly baseline, which fall an order of magnitude short, at 0.3 and 0.6\% on the two tests. That direction is the harmless one, since a conservative layer flags fewer features than expected rather than more.

Reproducibility outside the group that built the baselines is a second and independent property, and it is established by re-running either pipeline from deposited raw reads, which recovers the percentiles published by the resource each reproduces. Across the 61 MGnify samples so re-processed the median Spearman $\rho$ is lowest at the family and genus ranks, 0.86 and 0.88, and rises to between 0.98 and 1.00 on the functional layers; in 34 cMD3 samples it is 1.00 on every layer. A baseline built here can therefore be checked against numbers published elsewhere, which leaves open the question of what its percentiles are worth as evidence.

\subsection{What predicts is which features deviate, not how much}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Deviation from the reference is informative per feature, not in aggregate.} The fraction outside, the one summary a reference range yields with no labels at any stage, is beaten even by GMHI; a sparse model over the percentiles behind it reaches 0.720 and matches GMWI2 without that index's overlap with the test studies. Each bar is the mean AUROC over the left-out studies, with one dot per study; every checkGM and raw-abundance bar is the same L1-penalized logistic regression, differing only in its input, and each left-out study was scored by a model that never saw it. GMHI and GMWI2 are evaluated as published scores, without retraining. The deviation score plotted is the percentile-only model the software distributes; the pre-specified variant is reported in the text alone. \emph{Fraction outside} is the share of a sample's features beyond the reference range, scored directly and fitted on nothing, and is the only entry here that uses no labels at any stage. 0.5 is chance. $\dagger$GMWI2 was trained on samples from 10 of these 14 studies, so the four-study panel is its fair comparison.}
\label{fig:benchmark}
\end{figure*}

A reference range makes two different things available, and they are worth separating before either is compared with anything. The first is a single number: the share of a sample's features lying outside the range, its fraction outside, which needs no labels at any stage and is the summary a reference range yields on its own. The second is the vector of percentiles behind it. Scored directly on the fourteen read-pipeline case/control studies, the fraction outside reaches a mean AUROC of 0.564, on the species layer where it does best; the family and genus layers give 0.519 and 0.530. It is beaten by GMHI (11 of 14 studies, $p = 0.049$), and beaten decisively by a sparse model over the very percentiles it summarises, which reaches 0.720 and is higher in 13 of the 14 studies ($p = 0.0006$).

That gap is the central quantitative result of this paper. Deviation from a reference, taken as a quantity, barely separates cases from controls; the same deviations read feature by feature separate them well. The reason is that collapsing to one number discards which features moved and in which direction, and it is precisely there that the disease information sits: a sample depleted in \textit{Lachnospiraceae} and a sample enriched in something irrelevant have the same fraction outside. What a reference range contributes is therefore not a measure of overall abnormality but a statement about which features are abnormal --- which is what the rest of this paper is about, and what no single score, this one included, can deliver.

Read that way, the comparisons with published indices are calibration rather than contest. The deviation score built on the percentiles reaches 0.720 against 0.615 for GMHI and 0.476 for alpha diversity, which does not beat chance on these studies (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}). Against GMWI2 the two are level, 0.720 against 0.725, higher in 7 of 14 studies at $p = 0.72$, and the numbers should not be set against each other directly: matching sequencing-run accessions and ENA projects shows that GMWI2's training set contains samples from 10 of these 14 studies, whereas the score reported here was pre-specified and every study was scored by a model trained without it. The only clean comparison is the four studies neither model has seen, on which the deviation score reaches 0.634 against 0.608. Four studies cannot separate two classifiers and no separation is claimed. The claim is that a state-of-the-art index is matched by a score whose every feature can be inspected, which is the price the rest of the paper is arguing is worth paying.

Nor is the interpretation bought with accuracy. Because a percentile is a transformation of the profile rather than an addition to it, the possibility to rule out is a loss of predictive power, and none is found: trained on the raw centred log-ratios instead of their percentiles, on the same samples and the same features, the identical model reaches 0.705 against the percentiles' 0.720 (higher in 8 of 14 studies, $p = 0.30$). The transformation is free in accuracy and not free in parsimony, the percentile model keeping a median of 104 non-zero coefficients against 204 for the raw one. Nothing is added by the functional layers either: refitting on the 8{,}465 samples that carry HUMAnN tables gives 0.720 for the taxonomic percentiles alone against 0.692 once gene families and pathways are included, so on the read pipeline the taxonomic percentiles are doing the work.

A pre-specified variant of the score reaches more than this, and is not the one distributed. Adding the presence of each species to the percentiles lifts the mean AUROC to 0.746, but the lift cannot be trusted, and the reason was tested against a control rather than argued away: processing artifacts are inherited by presence features. The presence of \textit{[Collinsella] massiliensis} tracks study rather than disease, since it is absent from every case of nine cMD3 studies, from both arms of five more, and present in 85\% of the cases but none of the controls of another, and that incoherence is seized upon by the model as its strongest single feature. A second control bears on the same question: study identity could stand in for the label, since 15 of the 37 training studies are made up of cases or of controls but not of both, and training restricted to the 22 studies containing both reaches 0.723 for the percentile-only score and 0.718 for the full one. About three points of AUROC therefore separate the two variants on studies seen in training, and on the four studies GMWI2 never saw the ordering reverses, the percentile-only variant reaching 0.634 against 0.620. Since a feature that cannot be inspected cannot be trusted, and a presence feature standing in for a sequencing centre is exactly that, the percentile-only variant is what the software applies and what Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark} plots; the full score is reported here because it was pre-specified, not because it is recommended.

\subsection{The reference ranges recapitulate known disease associations}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig4.pdf}

(a) \cdiff{} infection, one dot per patient per feature, coloured by its call; open diamond, the study's 14 controls at their median; open ring with a count, the patients in whom the feature is absent. The label beside each row gives the finding that put it there --- the share of patients above the range, below it, or lacking the feature --- and within-range dots are drawn small and pale, since half of every row is one and the panel is not about them. (b) Nine colorectal cancer cohorts, each cohort's case median against its own control median, for the taxa, pathways and gene families with the largest consistent shifts; the label gives that shift in percentile points and the number of cohorts holding it, and every one of the twelve runs downward. \label{fig:known}

Since the deviation score and GMWI2 tie on prediction, whatever distinguishes them must lie in what their output means: a sample is reported by the published index to look diseased, while what is unusual within that sample is named by the reference ranges. In the \cdiff{} infection cohort of the assembly pipeline (56 patients, 14 controls) the loss of anaerobic colonization resistance that defines this infection is resolved family by family rather than as a single diversity deficit. \textit{Oscillospiraceae} lies below the reference range in 54\% of patients and in none of the controls. \textit{Atopobiaceae} is expected but missing in 59\% of patients against 21\% of controls, and six further anaerobe families follow. In the other direction, \textit{Lactobacillaceae}, \textit{Enterococcaceae} and \textit{Streptococcaceae} rise above the range in roughly one patient in eight and in no control (Fig.~\ref{fig:known}a). The twelve families and five gene families displayed were selected by how far they separate cases from controls, but the per-sample calls themselves require no control group, and the same report is therefore produced for a patient who has none (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c). Because the panel plots percentiles against the reference band rather than counts of calls, the taxa and the gene families are read on one axis and in one geometry, the same the single-sample report uses. One line of it makes the case for the scale: \textit{Enterobacteriaceae} is found 233$\times$ above the reference median on that same centred log-ratio scale yet inside the reference range, at the 87th percentile, a distinction no fold-change against a cohort mean could draw. The same line bounds the claim, since the reference range for that family spans more than five orders of magnitude; a call of within is a statement about the reference, not an assurance that the value is unremarkable.

What holds within one cohort holds across many. The depletion of fibre-fermenting commensals reported by meta-analyses of the same studies~\cite{thomas2019,wirbel2019} is recovered in the nine colorectal cancer cohorts of the read pipeline, and recovered in a form that can be read off a single axis. Six families shift consistently and every one of them shifts downward, led by \textit{Lachnospiraceae} in four cohorts at a median of $-35$ points, with \textit{Oscillospiraceae} ($-33$) and \textit{Bifidobacteriaceae} ($-29$) in three and \textit{Butyricicoccaceae} ($-29$) in two. Among the genera, \textit{Lachnospira} and \textit{Fusicatenibacter} fall in three cohorts apiece ($-25$) and eight further genera in two (Fig.~\ref{fig:known}b). Two of the six families are not fibre fermenters at all, \textit{Eggerthellaceae} ($-23$ points in three cohorts) and \textit{Streptococcaceae} ($-21$ in two), so what is recovered runs a little broader than the published pattern. Because the unit is a percentile of one baseline, those numbers may be read across the columns of the panel; and because gene families and pathways share that same scale, they may be read down it as well. Consistent shifts are found in 99 MetaCyc pathways and 82 KEGG orthologs, 86 and 72 of them downward, with thiamin formation and the S-adenosyl-L-methionine cycle among the strongest. The panel draws the largest of those shifts, all of which are depletions, so the thirteen pathways and ten orthologs that rise are left to the tables.

\subsection{Shifts from the reference: Hadza hunter-gatherers}

A reference range is a claim about a population, and the honest test of such a claim is a healthy population from which it was not built. Both baselines are dominated by European, North American and East Asian cohorts, and one constituent study is conspicuous in the calibration: the Hadza hunter-gatherers of Tanzania, sequenced alongside Nepali and Californian comparison groups~\cite{carter2023}. Scored out of sample, a median of 20.6\% of families is placed outside the reference range for Hadza samples against 4.4\% for other reference adults, whereas the 12 US participants of that same study, processed identically, sit at 11.5\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:population}a). Somewhat more than half of the 16-point excess over other reference adults is therefore attributable to the population rather than to the protocol, since 7 of those 16 points are reproduced by the twelve US participants themselves. A 9-to-7 split estimated from twelve donors is not a precise one, and a protocol contribution of some size is beyond doubt.

The genera responsible are named as well, which no single score could do (Fig.~\ref{fig:population}b). \textit{Treponema} is placed above the range in 71\% of Hadza samples, \textit{Sarcina} in 47\%, \textit{Anaerovibrio} in 42\% and \textit{Brachyspira} in 39\%, while \textit{Parasutterella}, \textit{Rikenella} and \textit{Sanguibacteroides} fall below the range in 26 to 33\% of them and are absent from a further 20 to 31\%, so that each is low or missing in 46 to 59\%. The gene families move with the taxa: aspartate kinase falls below the range in 62\% of samples and lysozyme rises above it in 33\%. These are the industrialization-depleted taxa for which the Hadza microbiome is known~\cite{carter2023}, recovered as deviations from an industrialized range rather than from a matched control group. The result is a caution and a method at once, since the fraction outside ceases to measure ill health once a sample comes from a population the baseline was not built from, and it is the per-feature output that tells the two apart.

One cohort cannot say how general that caution is, and the read baseline can, since its 6{,}810 reference adults come from 22 studies in 17 countries and each is scored against a baseline rebuilt without its own study (Fig.~\ref{fig:population}b). The honest answer is narrower than the Hadza result suggests. Study-to-study variation within a single country is almost as large as variation between countries, 1.85 against 2.04 points on families, so most of the spread across populations cannot be told apart from the protocols that produced it. Two populations clear that bar: Madagascar and Mongolia sit five points above the industrialized median of 4.4\% on families, about three within-country standard deviations, and five to six above it on genera; on species the excess falls to three points for Madagascar while Mongolia holds at five. Theirs is the only excess that replicates across all three ranks. A third, India, is elevated on families alone, at 8.6\%, but it is scored on 16 assessed families where a single feature is six points and it sits exactly at the industrialized median on species, so it is not counted. The surprise runs the other way: Japan and China are at the industrialized level on families and two to five points above it on genus and species. A reference range can therefore transfer at one rank and fail at another, which no reasoning about which cohorts are industrialized would have predicted, and which only a baseline reporting every rank separately can reveal. It is also the clearest illustration of the fourth claim made in the introduction: a list of named, directional shifts is what turns an unexplained score into a set of candidates for follow-up.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary}

checkGM places a single gut metagenome among reference adults processed the same way, and ships that reference with the software producing it. Its central quantitative result is a gap between two readings of the same output: the fraction of a sample's features outside the reference range reaches 0.564 as a classifier, while a sparse model over the percentiles behind it reaches 0.720 and matches a published health index without inheriting that index's overlap with the test studies. Deviation from a reference is informative per feature and not in aggregate, and that is the argument for reporting every feature --- which the tool then does, stating which taxa, genes and pathways are unusual and by how much. One sample can accordingly be read without a control group, all samples are placed on one scale, and the baseline travels as statistics alone.

\subsection{Limitations}

\emph{What a reference range is, and is not.} The definition adopted here is a label in public metadata, not verified health, and that is the weakest link in the construction: a baseline of adults who are merely not recorded as ill is not the same object as a clinically screened reference population, and no amount of downstream care repairs it. The range is also only as wide as the cohorts behind it. Both baselines are dominated by European, North American and East Asian studies, and the Hadza result shows what follows --- a fraction outside of 20.6\% against 4.4\% for a population that is not ill but merely unrepresented. How far that generalizes is now measured rather than assumed, across the 17 countries of the read baseline, and the measurement bounds the claim in both directions: two populations misfit at every rank, while most of the variation between countries cannot be separated from the variation between studies of one country. Population and protocol remain confounded, and a single cohort can establish neither. A reference range should accordingly be read as a statement about one population measured one way, and re-derived for the population actually being tested, as clinical reference intervals are. Two further consequences of the definition are visible in the results. Taxa that are enriched in colorectal cancer but rare in reference adults, among them \textit{Fusobacterium nucleatum} and \textit{Parvimonas}, fall below the prevalence filter and receive no percentile at all, since a reference range can speak only about the features the reference population carries. And the recovery of known biology is partial: in the two inflammatory bowel disease cohorts of the assembly pipeline, \textit{Blautia} is found higher in cases rather than lower, and \textit{Faecalibacterium} is higher in both as well, significantly in one of them, against the well-replicated depletion of each~\cite{lloydprice2019,franzosa2019}. Neither finding contradicts the colorectal cancer result above, which was obtained on the other pipeline and in other cohorts, but neither is reassuring. On the four studies neither model had seen, the deviation score only ties GMWI2.

\emph{The analysis pipeline is part of the measurement.} A second limitation follows from shipping two pipelines rather than one. Fifteen samples in these cohorts were measured both ways, and scoring each measurement against its own baseline shows that the two reports agree on the ordering of a sample's taxa but not on its individual calls. The median Spearman correlation between the two sets of percentiles is 0.71 for families and 0.62 for genera, yet the features each pipeline places outside the reference range overlap barely above chance: across 381 shared family assessments the assembly pipeline flags 19 and the read pipeline 69, of which 8 are the same feature against 4.5 expected if the two flagged independently. A percentile is therefore a statement about a sample as measured, not about the sample in itself, and the two baselines must not be treated as interchangeable at the level of a single feature. This is the reason the range and the software are distributed as one object, and the reason a baseline from one pipeline may never be used to score output from the other.

Reference intervals became useful in clinical chemistry once they were defined per assay, published with the method, and re-derived for the populations actually being tested. checkGM is an attempt to meet those three requirements for gut metagenomes.

\section{Code and Data Availability}

checkGM is a Python package under the MIT license, distributed from \url{https://github.com/khoa-yelo/Meta2} together with the code, container recipes for both pipelines and the documentation. Five commands cover the workflow: \texttt{setup} fetches a baseline, \texttt{profile} runs either pipeline on raw reads, \texttt{assess} places a sample against its baseline and writes its percentiles, calls and per-sample report figure, \texttt{score} applies the deviation score, and \texttt{end-to-end} chains the three. Assessing a profile someone else measured needs only the baseline, the 52 or 62~MiB above; the reference databases, 90~GB for the assembly pipeline, are needed only to measure raw reads. The baselines used here (assembly v0.8, read v0.2) and the container image are deposited on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23181418); the archive also carries a more strictly filtered variant of each, which no result in this paper uses.
