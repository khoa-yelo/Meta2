\title{checkGM: A Reference-Based Framework\\for Interpreting the Gut Microbiome}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{[Authors]}\IEEEauthorblockA{[Affiliation]}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Gut metagenome profiling reports hundreds of abundances without stating which of them are unusual, because the field has no reference against which an individual value can be judged. checkGM is a reference-based framework that supplies one. Two standard analysis pipelines are distributed together with baselines of healthy adults processed identically, so that every taxon, gene family and pathway of a new sample is reported as a percentile among those adults, with a call of within, below or above the healthy range. MGnify v5 is reproduced by the assembly pipeline (1{,}941 adults, 26 studies) and curatedMetagenomicData~3 by the read pipeline (6{,}494 adults, 19 studies), and each baseline travels as summary statistics alone. The framework is shown to be approximately calibrated: in three held-out cohorts per pipeline, healthy adults fall outside the healthy range at a median of 5\% of families in the assembly pipeline and 7\% in the read pipeline, against the 5\% expected by construction. The percentiles are also shown to cost nothing in predictive power. A pre-specified sparse score built on the percentiles matches the published Gut Microbiome Wellness Index~2 without that index's overlap with the test studies, using half as many features as the same model trained on raw abundances. What the reference adds is interpretation. The loss of obligate anaerobes is read directly off individual \cdiff{} patients, and the depletion of fibre-fermenting commensals is recovered across nine colorectal cancer cohorts on one common scale. The taxa that place Hadza hunter-gatherers outside an industrialized range are named rather than merely scored, which bounds the reference as much as it demonstrates it, since calibration holds only for the populations a baseline was built from.
\end{abstract}

gut microbiome, metagenomics, reference ranges, interpretability, reproducible pipelines

\section{Introduction}

Any clinical measurement that is acted upon is interpreted against a reference interval, the spread of values observed in healthy people measured on the same assay~\cite{horn2003}. No comparable interval exists for the gut metagenome. Hundreds of taxa, gene families and pathways are quantified by current profilers~\cite{beghini2021,richardson2023}, yet whether any value among them is unusual is decided separately by each study, against whichever controls it happened to recruit. An isolated profile therefore cannot be interpreted at all, since the comparison group it would require is absent, and effect sizes obtained in different studies cannot be placed on a common axis, a long-recognised obstacle to meta-analysis~\cite{duvallet2017}. The usual workaround, in which a profile is condensed into a single health index~\cite{gupta2020,chang2024}, buys comparability at a price: the resulting number reports that a sample looks diseased without saying which of its features are unusual, or by how much.

The gap cannot be blamed on a shortage of healthy data, since tens of thousands of healthy gut metagenomes have been deposited~\cite{richardson2023,antonello2025}, but is explained by two properties of the data that exist. A range is valid only for measurements produced the same way, because profiles from different pipelines are not interchangeable~\cite{sczyrba2017}, so a range and its software must be distributed as one object; and public cohorts are released as per-sample tables that must be re-processed before anything new can be compared against them. What has been missing is consequently not another healthy cohort but a pipeline carrying its own baseline.

checkGM is the framework proposed here to supply exactly that (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). Two public resources are reproduced by its pipelines, MGnify v5~\cite{richardson2023} by an assembly-based one and curatedMetagenomicData~3 (cMD3)~\cite{pasolli2017} by a read-based one, each carrying a baseline of healthy adults drawn from the resource it reproduces and held as summary statistics alone: no samples, a few megabytes. Once raw reads are supplied, every feature is returned with its percentile among healthy adults and a call of within, below or above the healthy range; alongside these, the features most healthy adults carry but this sample lacks are listed. The framework is assessed below against three requirements, taken in turn. It must be calibrated, or the ranges mean nothing; it must not cost predictive power, or interpretation would be bought at the expense of accuracy; and it must deliver what a single score cannot, namely a statement of what is unusual in a given sample.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Cohorts and pipelines}

\emph{Assembly pipeline.} Of 19{,}437 MGnify v5 analyses inventoried from 130 studies, 17{,}780 were of human metagenomes, and 9{,}195 gut metagenomes were retained from those, one analysis per sample across 69 studies. Of these, 8{,}742 passed assembly quality filters of at least 5~Mb assembled and 20 of 52 single-copy marker genes detected (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}b). Health status, age and sex were obtained from a manual curation of the study and sample metadata. So that a new sample can be placed on exactly the scale its baseline was built on, the MGnify v5 workflow is reproduced from raw reads inside a pinned container (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}a). Assembly is performed by metaSPAdes 3.15.3~\cite{nurk2017} or MEGAHIT 1.2.9~\cite{li2015}, genes are called by Prodigal 2.6.3~\cite{hyatt2010} and FragGeneScan~\cite{rho2010}, taxonomy is assigned by DIAMOND~\cite{buchfink2015} against UniRef90, and function by eggNOG-mapper~\cite{huerta2017}, KOfam~\cite{aramaki2020} and InterProScan~\cite{jones2014}. Taxa are expressed as coverage-weighted proportions of assigned sequence, and genes as copies per genome scaled by the coverage of the marker genes~\cite{nayfach2015}.

\emph{Read pipeline.} From the 9{,}246 cMD3 MetaPhlAn~3 profiles of 37 studies and their curated metadata~\cite{antonello2025}, 7{,}478 adult stool controls were retained, each with a profile at least 90\% resolvable to an NCBI family. Profiling is performed by MetaPhlAn 3.0.14~\cite{beghini2021} with the mpa\_v30 markers on untrimmed reads, as in cMD3, and HUMAnN~3 gene-family and pathway tables may be added, grouped into KEGG orthologs (KOs) and MetaCyc pathways~\cite{caspi2020}.

\subsection{Baselines}

Both baselines are constructed by the same rules. Compositional layers are transformed to centred log-ratios~\cite{aitchison1982} over the features present in at least 5\% of baseline samples. For each surviving feature, its prevalence within each sequencing-depth class and its 1st--99th percentiles among the samples that carry it are stored, with studies weighted equally so that the largest cohorts cannot dominate the range. Percentiles are given only where at least 20 detections support them.

Two inclusion tiers were built for each pipeline. Both require a healthy or control label and exclude any sample labelled diseased or under treatment. The \emph{strict} tier additionally requires that the label be present rather than merely uncontradicted. It further requires, wherever the corresponding field is recorded, that the donor be aged 18--65, carry a body-mass index between 18.5 and 30, not be pregnant and report no recent antibiotics. Strictness costs samples, 1{,}588 against 1{,}941 adults in the assembly baseline and 6{,}235 against 6{,}494 in the read baseline, and the lenient tier is the one used throughout; the strict tier is kept as a sensitivity analysis.

Scoring inverts those same rules. A new sample is normalized exactly as the baseline samples were, and its percentile for each feature is obtained by interpolation between the stored quantiles. A feature is then called \textbf{low} or \textbf{high} when it falls outside the 2.5th--97.5th percentile range, and \textbf{expected but missing} when it is carried by at least 70\% of healthy samples of its depth class but not by the sample at hand. The share of a sample's features lying outside the range, its \emph{fraction outside}, should be about 5\% when the sample is healthy, and it is that expectation which makes the baselines testable. Because only these summaries are kept, never the samples from which they were derived, a bundle occupies a few megabytes and can be redistributed where the cohort behind it could not be.

\subsection{Evaluation sets and leakage control}

Three disjoint sets were fixed before any sample was scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}b). The baselines themselves hold 1{,}941 healthy adults from 26 studies in the assembly pipeline and 6{,}494 from 19 studies in the read pipeline. Three further healthy studies per pipeline, 233 and 317 adults, were held out entirely, and are used only to test calibration. The disease studies form the case/control sets: 2{,}294 samples from 11 studies in the assembly pipeline and 1{,}437 from 15 studies in the read pipeline. Of the latter, the 14 studies with at least 20 cases and 20 controls enter the classification benchmark, the fifteenth being small enough that it is used only for the disease-shift analyses. Together the three assembly-pipeline sets hold 4{,}468 of the 8{,}742 samples that pass quality control, drawn from 40 of the 68 studies that survive it; the remainder is scored nowhere below.

Everything reported below rests on one condition: no sample was ever compared with a baseline that contained it. Baseline studies were accordingly scored against a baseline rebuilt without them, and the cases and controls of a disease study were always scored against the same baseline, so that a difference between them cannot be an artifact of the reference. Thirty samples deposited in two cMD3 studies were scored against a baseline from which their duplicate had been removed, and were never allowed to fall on opposite sides of a training and test split. With the splits fixed in this way, every comparison that follows may be read as out-of-sample throughout.

\subsection{Statistics and comparisons}

The \emph{shift} of a feature in a case/control study is defined as the median percentile of the cases minus that of the controls of the same study. A shift is called significant when it reaches at least 10 points at $q \leq 0.05$ (Mann--Whitney with Benjamini--Hochberg correction~\cite{benjamini1995}), and \emph{consistent} when it holds in the same direction in at least two studies of one condition.

For the benchmark, each left-out study was classified by a random forest~\cite{breiman2001} trained on all the other case/control studies. Two choices were varied independently: the representation, which was either the percentiles, the raw abundances or alpha diversity; and, for the percentiles, the layer, which was the taxa, the gene families and pathways, or the two together. Paired AUROC comparisons were made by a Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the left-out studies, one-sided wherever a direction had been pre-specified and two-sided elsewhere. This benchmark falls in the first class, and the comparisons that set the supervised score described below against another model fall in the second, no direction having been fixed in advance for those. Where the observed difference runs in the pre-specified direction a one-sided value is half its two-sided equivalent; the 0.33 and 0.30 reported below for the matched sparse models would accordingly read 0.16 and 0.15 one-sided.

GMHI~\cite{gupta2020} and GMWI2~\cite{chang2024} were scored on the read pipeline exactly as published, without retraining. Neither index is met on wholly equal terms. GMHI is defined on species, so on the assembly pipeline, whose taxonomy is resolved to genus, it could only be approximated from the ratio of its health-associated to its health-depleted genera; and even on the read pipeline only 33 of its 50 species are matched by name in MetaPhlAn~3. Both figures should therefore be read as lower bounds on what that index achieves on its native input.

So that those indices would be met on their own ground, a sparse health score on checkGM output was pre-specified: an L1-penalized logistic regression on the percentiles, the fraction outside, the number of expected-but-missing features and the presence of each species. Model and features were fixed before any result was seen, each study was scored by a model trained without it, and the penalty was selected by grouped cross-validation inside the training studies alone.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The baselines are calibrated and the pipelines reproduce their sources}

If the stored quantiles describe healthy adults, then healthy adults a baseline has never seen should fall outside its range about 5\% of the time, and that expectation is met on both pipelines. On the three held-out cohorts per pipeline, the only wholly independent test, a median of 4.9\% of families is placed outside the range by the assembly baseline and 7.1\% by the read baseline. The larger, semi-independent test in which each baseline study is scored against a baseline rebuilt without it gives 5.4\% and 3.9\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}c). The agreement is not uniform across layers, since every layer but one spans 2.6 to 7.2\% around the nominal 5\%, the read pipeline's pathways and orthologs sitting on the conservative side of it under the semi-independent test. The exception is the KEGG modules of the assembly baseline, which fall an order of magnitude short, at 0.3 and 0.6\% on the two tests. That direction is the harmless one, since a conservative layer flags fewer features than expected rather than more. Reproducibility outside the group that built the baselines is a second and independent property, and it is established by re-running either pipeline from deposited raw reads, which recovers the percentiles published by the resource each reproduces. Across the 61 MGnify samples so re-processed the median Spearman $\rho$ is lowest at the family and genus ranks, 0.86 and 0.88, and rises to between 0.98 and 1.00 on the functional layers; in 34 cMD3 samples it is 1.00 on every layer (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}d). A baseline built here can therefore be checked against numbers published elsewhere, which leaves open the question of what its percentiles are worth as evidence.

\subsection{Interpretability is not paid for in predictive power}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{In studies left out of training, checkGM output is competitive with published health indices.} Violet, checkGM output; 0.5 is chance; the percentile-only and restricted-training variants of the health score are reported in the text alone. $\dagger$GMWI2 was trained on samples from 10 of these 14 studies, so the right-hand panel is its fair comparison.}
\label{fig:benchmark}
\end{figure*}

Because a percentile is a transformation of the profile rather than an addition to it, the first possibility to rule out is a loss of predictive power, and none is found (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}). Across 11 assembly-pipeline studies, each left out in turn, a mean AUROC of 0.62 is reached by the percentiles against 0.58 by raw abundances (higher in 9 of 11 studies, $p = 0.021$), 0.51 by alpha diversity and 0.48 by a genus-level GMHI approximation. The ordering is the same over 14 read-pipeline studies, where 0.69 is reached against 0.66 for raw abundances (10 of 14, $p = 0.039$) and 0.62 for the published GMHI (11 of 14, $p = 0.008$). Nor does the result depend on the inclusion tier: repeated against the strict assembly baseline, the benchmark gives 0.615 for the percentiles against 0.581 for raw abundances (7 of 11 studies, $p = 0.051$). Comparable information is carried by the gene families and pathways alone (0.67), but nothing is added on top of the taxa (0.69 either way). Refitting the supervised score with them changes nothing either, at 0.73 without the functional layers against 0.72 with them. That comparison runs on the 8{,}465 samples carrying HUMAnN tables, which is why both values sit below the 0.75 of Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}. On the read pipeline the taxonomic percentiles are therefore doing the work. The ordering reverses on the assembly pipeline, whose genus-level taxonomy is coarse enough that genes alone reach 0.63 against 0.59 for the taxa alone, and the two together at 0.62; all three are plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}.

How much of that margin belongs to the percentiles themselves, rather than to the forest that consumes them, is worth separating out. When the same L1-penalised model is trained on raw centred log-ratios instead, most of the difference disappears: 0.719 against 0.705 for the sparse model (higher in 7 of 14 studies, $p = 0.33$) and 0.747 against 0.744 once the summaries and species presence are added (10 of 14, $p = 0.30$). The advantage over raw abundances is therefore classifier-specific rather than general, and percentiles are found to predict about as well as the abundances they are derived from on half as many features, a median of 104 non-zero coefficients against 205. What the percentiles add is accordingly not accuracy but an output that can be read, which is the subject of the rest of this paper.

The harder comparison is with GMWI2, which reaches 0.72 on the same 14 studies against 0.75 for the health score (higher in 10 of 14, $p = 0.22$). Those numbers should not be set against each other directly, and the reason is more informative than the gap between them. Matching sequencing-run accessions and ENA projects shows that GMWI2's training set contains samples from 10 of these 14 studies, whereas the score reported here was pre-specified and every study was scored by a model trained without it. The only clean comparison is therefore the four studies neither model has seen, on which the two are level at 0.62 against 0.61. Two classifiers cannot be separated by four studies and no such separation is claimed; the claim is narrower, that the accuracy of a state-of-the-art index is reached by a score whose every feature can be inspected.

Two cautions attach to that score, and each was tested against a control rather than argued away. The first is that processing artifacts are inherited by its species-presence features: \textit{[Collinsella] massiliensis} is present in most controls and absent from every case of three cMD3 studies, and that batch difference is seized upon by the model as its strongest single feature. The second is that study identity could stand in for the label, since 15 of the 37 training studies are made up of cases or of controls but not of both. About three points of AUROC are lost to each control: a percentile-only variant, to which the presence features are invisible, reaches 0.719, and training restricted to the 22 studies that contain both cases and controls reaches 0.717, against 0.747 for the full score. Both figures are level with the 0.725 of GMWI2 on these same studies rather than above it, so the margin reported in the previous paragraph should be read as the unrestricted score's and not as one that survives either control. What is established by the controls is that the artifacts are not what the accuracy rests on, since on the four studies GMWI2 never saw the percentile-only variant holds up, at 0.634 against 0.619 for the full score and 0.608 for GMWI2.

\subsection{Reading a single sample against the reference}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig4.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Reference ranges reproduce known disease associations, in one cohort and across nine.} (a) \cdiff{} infection: for the twelve families that separate cases from controls most, the share of the 56 patients in whom each is below the healthy range, expected but missing, or above it; diamonds, the same share among the study's 14 controls. (b) Colorectal cancer: shift in median percentile between cases and the controls of the same cohort, for the families, genera, MetaCyc pathways and KEGG orthologs carrying the largest consistent shifts rather than every consistent one; dots mark significant shifts. Because every row is a percentile of the same baseline, $-30$ points means the same thing in every column and on every layer.}
\label{fig:known}
\end{figure*}

Since the health score and GMWI2 tie on prediction, whatever distinguishes them must lie in what their output means: a sample is reported by the published index to look diseased, while what is unusual within that sample is named by the reference ranges. In the \cdiff{} infection cohort of the assembly pipeline (56 patients, 14 controls) the loss of anaerobic colonization resistance that defines this infection is resolved family by family rather than as a single diversity deficit. \textit{Oscillospiraceae} lies below the healthy range in 54\% of patients and in none of the controls. \textit{Atopobiaceae} is expected but missing in 59\% of patients against 21\% of controls, and six further anaerobe families follow. In the other direction, \textit{Lactobacillaceae}, \textit{Enterococcaceae} and \textit{Streptococcaceae} rise above the range in roughly one patient in eight and in no control (Fig.~\ref{fig:known}a). The twelve families displayed were selected by how far they separate cases from controls, but the per-sample calls themselves require no control group, and the same report is therefore produced for a patient who has none (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c). One line of it makes the case for the scale: \textit{Enterobacteriaceae} is found 233$\times$ above the typical healthy adult yet inside the healthy range, at the 87th percentile, a distinction no fold-change against a cohort mean could draw. The same line bounds the claim, since the healthy range for that family spans more than five orders of magnitude; a call of within is a statement about the reference, not an assurance that the value is unremarkable.

What holds within one cohort holds across many. The depletion of fibre-fermenting commensals reported by meta-analyses of the same studies~\cite{thomas2019,wirbel2019} is recovered in the nine colorectal cancer cohorts of the read pipeline, and recovered in a form that can be read off a single axis. Six families shift consistently and every one of them shifts downward, led by \textit{Lachnospiraceae} in four cohorts at a median of $-35$ points, with \textit{Oscillospiraceae} ($-33$) and \textit{Bifidobacteriaceae} ($-29$) in three and \textit{Butyricicoccaceae} ($-29$) in two. Among the genera, \textit{Lachnospira} and \textit{Fusicatenibacter} fall in three cohorts apiece ($-25$) and eight further genera in two (Fig.~\ref{fig:known}b). Two of the six families are not fibre fermenters at all, \textit{Eggerthellaceae} ($-23$ points in three cohorts) and \textit{Streptococcaceae} ($-21$ in two), so what is recovered runs a little broader than the published pattern. Because the unit is a percentile of one baseline, those numbers may be read across the columns of the panel; and because gene families and pathways share that same scale, they may be read down it as well. Consistent shifts are found in 100 MetaCyc pathways and 80 KEGG orthologs, 85 and 70 of them downward, with thiamin formation and the S-adenosyl-L-methionine cycle among the strongest. The panel draws the largest of those shifts, all of which are depletions, so the fifteen pathways and ten orthologs that rise are left to the tables.

\subsection{A healthy population the baseline does not fit}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Hadza hunter-gatherers sit outside an industrialized healthy range, and the reference ranges name the genera responsible.} All samples are healthy adults, each scored against a baseline rebuilt without its own study. (a) Share of features outside the healthy range per sample. (b) The common gut genera and gene families that drive it, with the same study's US participants and the rest of the baseline for comparison.}
\label{fig:population}
\end{figure*}

A reference range is a claim about a population, and the honest test of such a claim is a healthy population from which it was not built. Both baselines are dominated by European, North American and East Asian cohorts, and one constituent study is conspicuous in the calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}c): the Hadza hunter-gatherers of Tanzania, sequenced alongside Nepali and Californian comparison groups~\cite{carter2023}. Scored out of sample, a median of 20.6\% of families is placed outside the healthy range for Hadza samples against 4.4\% for other healthy adults, whereas the 12 US participants of that same study, processed identically, sit at 11.5\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:population}a). Somewhat more than half of the 16-point excess over other healthy adults is therefore attributable to the population rather than to the protocol, since 7 of those 16 points are reproduced by the twelve US participants themselves. A 9-to-7 split estimated from twelve donors is not a precise one, and a protocol contribution of some size is beyond doubt.

The genera responsible are named as well, which no single score could do (Fig.~\ref{fig:population}b). \textit{Treponema} is placed above the range in 71\% of Hadza samples, \textit{Sarcina} in 47\%, \textit{Anaerovibrio} in 42\% and \textit{Brachyspira} in 39\%, while \textit{Parasutterella}, \textit{Rikenella} and \textit{Sanguibacteroides} are low or missing in 46 to 59\% of them. The gene families move with the taxa: aspartate kinase falls below the range in 62\% of samples and lysozyme rises above it in 33\%. These are the industrialization-depleted taxa for which the Hadza microbiome is known~\cite{carter2023}, recovered as deviations from an industrialized range rather than from a matched control group. The result is a caution and a method at once, since the fraction outside ceases to measure ill health once a sample comes from a population the baseline was not built from, and it is the per-feature output that tells the two apart.

\section{Discussion}

checkGM places a single gut metagenome among healthy adults processed the same way, and ships that reference with the software producing it. Treated as a predictor, a score built on this output matches a published health index without inheriting that index's overlap with the test studies; treated as a report, it states which taxa, genes and pathways are unusual and by how much. One sample can accordingly be read without a control group, all samples are placed on one scale, and the baseline travels as statistics alone.

The limits are visible in the same figures. On the four studies neither model had seen, the score only ties GMWI2, and the recovery of known biology is in any case partial. In the two inflammatory bowel disease cohorts of the assembly pipeline, \textit{Blautia} is found higher in cases rather than lower, and \textit{Faecalibacterium} is higher in both as well, significantly in one of them, against the well-replicated depletion of each~\cite{lloydprice2019,franzosa2019}. Neither finding contradicts the colorectal cancer result above, which was obtained on the other pipeline and in other cohorts, but neither is reassuring. Taxa that are enriched in colorectal cancer but rare in healthy adults, among them \textit{Fusobacterium nucleatum} and \textit{Parvimonas}, fall below the prevalence filter and receive no percentile at all, since a reference range can speak only about the features healthy people carry. The Hadza result bounds the claim geographically, and population cannot in either cohort be separated from study.

checkGM is a Python package under the MIT license, distributed from \url{https://github.com/khoa-yelo/Meta2} together with the code, container recipes for both pipelines and the documentation. The lenient baselines (assembly v0.8, read v0.2) and the container image are deposited on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23181418), the strict tier being rebuilt from the archived configuration by the same script.

Reference intervals became useful in clinical chemistry once they were defined per assay, published with the method, and re-derived for the populations actually being tested. checkGM is an attempt to meet those three requirements for gut metagenomes.
