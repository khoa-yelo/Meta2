\title{Healthy reference ranges for gut metagenomes,\\shipped with the pipelines that measure them}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{[Authors]}\IEEEauthorblockA{[Affiliation]}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Hundreds of abundances are reported for every gut metagenome, and none of them is accompanied by a statement of what is normal. In clinical chemistry that statement is supplied by a reference interval measured in healthy people on the same assay; metagenomics has no equivalent, so normality is redefined by each study from its own controls and an isolated sample cannot be interpreted at all. checkGM is presented here as a remedy: two standard analysis pipelines are distributed together with baselines of healthy adults processed identically, and every taxon, gene family and pathway of a new sample is reported as a percentile among those adults. MGnify v5 is reproduced by the assembly pipeline (1{,}941 adults, 26 studies) and curatedMetagenomicData~3 by the read pipeline (6{,}494 adults, 19 studies). Healthy cohorts the baselines had never seen were found to fall outside the healthy range at close to the expected 5\%, and in studies held out of training cases were separated from controls more accurately by the percentiles than by raw abundances, a pre-specified sparse score built on them matching the published Gut Microbiome Wellness Index~2 without that index's overlap with the test studies. What distinguishes such percentiles from a score is that they explain themselves: the loss of obligate anaerobes is read off individual \cdiff{} patients, the depletion of butyrate producers across nine colorectal cancer cohorts, and the genera that place Hadza hunter-gatherers outside an industrialized healthy range.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
gut microbiome, metagenomics, reference ranges, interpretability, reproducible pipelines
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Any clinical measurement that is acted upon is interpreted against a reference interval, the spread of values observed in healthy people measured on the same assay~\cite{horn2003}. No comparable interval exists for the gut metagenome. Hundreds of taxa, gene families and pathways are quantified by current profilers~\cite{beghini2021,richardson2023}, yet whether any value among them is unusual is decided separately by each study, against whichever controls it happened to recruit. An isolated profile therefore cannot be interpreted at all, since the comparison group it would require is absent, and effect sizes obtained in different studies cannot be placed on a common axis, a long-recognised obstacle to meta-analysis~\cite{duvallet2017}. The usual workaround, in which a profile is condensed into a single health index~\cite{gupta2020,chang2024}, buys comparability at a price: the resulting number reports that a sample looks diseased without saying which of its features are unusual, or by how much.

The gap cannot be blamed on a shortage of healthy data, since tens of thousands of healthy gut metagenomes have been deposited, but is explained by two properties of the data that exist. A range is valid only for measurements produced the same way, because profiles from different pipelines are not interchangeable~\cite{sczyrba2017}, so a range and its software must be distributed as one object; and public cohorts are released as per-sample tables that must be re-processed before anything new can be compared against them. What has been missing is consequently not another healthy cohort but a pipeline carrying its own baseline.

checkGM was built to supply exactly that (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). Two public resources are reproduced by its pipelines, MGnify v5~\cite{richardson2023} by an assembly-based one and curatedMetagenomicData~3 (cMD3)~\cite{pasolli2017} by a read-based one, each carrying a baseline of healthy adults drawn from the resource it reproduces and held as summary statistics alone: no samples, a few megabytes. Once raw reads are supplied, every feature is returned with its percentile among healthy adults, a call of within, below or above the healthy range, and the features most healthy adults carry but this sample lacks. Three claims are made for that output below and taken up in turn: that it is calibrated, that it carries as much disease information as a published index, and that, unlike a score, it says what is unusual.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Cohorts and pipelines}

\emph{Assembly pipeline.} Of 19{,}437 MGnify v5 analyses inventoried from 130 studies, 9{,}195 gut metagenomes were retained, one analysis per sample across 69 studies, and 8{,}742 of these passed assembly quality filters of at least 5~Mb assembled and 20 of 52 single-copy marker genes detected (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}b). Health status, age and sex were obtained from a manual curation of the study and sample metadata. So that a new sample can be placed on exactly the scale its baseline was built on, the MGnify v5 workflow is reproduced from raw reads inside a pinned container (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}a). Assembly is performed by metaSPAdes 3.15.3~\cite{nurk2017} or MEGAHIT 1.2.9~\cite{li2015}, genes are called by Prodigal 2.6.3~\cite{hyatt2010} and FragGeneScan~\cite{rho2010}, taxonomy is assigned by DIAMOND~\cite{buchfink2015} against UniRef90, and function by eggNOG-mapper~\cite{huerta2017}, KOfam~\cite{aramaki2020} and InterProScan~\cite{jones2014}. Taxa are expressed as coverage-weighted proportions of assigned sequence, and genes as copies per genome scaled by the coverage of the marker genes~\cite{nayfach2015}.

\emph{Read pipeline.} From the 9{,}246 cMD3 MetaPhlAn~3 profiles of 37 studies and their curated metadata~\cite{antonello2025}, 7{,}478 adult stool controls were retained, each with a profile at least 90\% resolvable to an NCBI family. Profiling is performed by MetaPhlAn 3.0.14~\cite{beghini2021} with the mpa\_v30 markers on untrimmed reads, as in cMD3, and HUMAnN~3 gene-family and pathway tables may be added, grouped into KEGG orthologs (KOs) and MetaCyc pathways~\cite{caspi2020}.

\subsection{Baselines}

Both baselines are constructed by the same rules. Compositional layers are transformed to centred log-ratios~\cite{aitchison1982} over the features present in at least 5\% of baseline samples. For each surviving feature, its prevalence within each sequencing-depth class and its 1st--99th percentiles among the samples that carry it are stored, with studies weighted equally so that the largest cohorts cannot dominate the range. Percentiles are given only where at least 20 detections support them.

Two inclusion tiers were built for each pipeline. Both require a healthy or control label and exclude any sample labelled diseased or under treatment; the \emph{strict} tier additionally requires that the label be present rather than merely uncontradicted and, wherever the corresponding field is recorded, that the donor be aged 18--65, have a body-mass index between 18.5 and 30, not be pregnant and report no recent antibiotics. Strictness costs samples, 1{,}588 against 1{,}941 adults in the assembly baseline and 6{,}235 against 6{,}494 in the read baseline, and the lenient tier is the one used throughout; the strict tier is kept as a sensitivity analysis.

Scoring inverts those same rules. A new sample is normalized exactly as the baseline samples were, and its percentile for each feature is obtained by interpolation between the stored quantiles. A feature is then called \textbf{low} or \textbf{high} when it falls outside the 2.5th--97.5th percentile range, and \textbf{expected but missing} when it is carried by at least 70\% of healthy samples of its depth class but not by the sample at hand. The share of a sample's features lying outside the range, its \emph{fraction outside}, should be about 5\% when the sample is healthy, and it is that expectation which makes the baselines testable. Because only these summaries are kept, never the samples from which they were derived, a bundle occupies a few megabytes and can be redistributed where the cohort behind it could not be.

\subsection{Evaluation sets and leakage control}

Three disjoint sets were fixed before any sample was scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}b). The baselines themselves hold 1{,}941 healthy adults from 26 studies in the assembly pipeline and 6{,}494 from 19 studies in the read pipeline. Three further healthy studies per pipeline, 233 and 317 adults, were held out entirely, and are used only to test calibration. The disease studies form the case/control sets: 2{,}294 samples from 11 studies in the assembly pipeline and 1{,}437 from 15 studies in the read pipeline. Of the latter, the 14 studies with at least 20 cases and 20 controls enter the classification benchmark, the fifteenth being small enough that it is used only for the disease-shift analyses.

Everything reported below rests on one condition: no sample was ever compared with a baseline that contained it. Baseline studies were accordingly scored against a baseline rebuilt without them, and the cases and controls of a disease study were always scored against the same baseline, so that a difference between them cannot be an artifact of the reference. Thirty samples deposited in two cMD3 studies were scored against a baseline from which their duplicate had been removed, and were never allowed to fall on opposite sides of a training and test split.

\subsection{Statistics and comparisons}

The \emph{shift} of a feature in a case/control study is defined as the median percentile of the cases minus that of the controls of the same study. A shift is called significant when it reaches at least 10 points at $q \leq 0.05$ (Mann--Whitney with Benjamini--Hochberg correction~\cite{benjamini1995}), and \emph{consistent} when it holds in the same direction in at least two studies of one condition.

For the benchmark, each left-out study was classified by a random forest~\cite{breiman2001} trained on all the other case/control studies. Two choices were varied independently: the representation, which was either the percentiles, the raw abundances or alpha diversity; and, for the percentiles, the layer, which was the taxa, the gene families and pathways, or the two together.

GMHI~\cite{gupta2020} and GMWI2~\cite{chang2024} were scored on the read pipeline exactly as published, without retraining. Neither index is met on wholly equal terms. GMHI is defined on species, so on the assembly pipeline, whose taxonomy is resolved to genus, it could only be approximated from the ratio of its health-associated to its health-depleted genera; and even on the read pipeline only 33 of its 50 species are matched by name in MetaPhlAn~3. Both figures should therefore be read as lower bounds on what that index achieves on its native input.

So that those indices would be met on their own ground, a sparse health score on checkGM output was pre-specified: an L1-penalized logistic regression on the percentiles, the fraction outside, the number of expected-but-missing features and the presence of each species. Model and features were fixed before any result was seen, each study was scored by a model trained without it, and the penalty was selected by grouped cross-validation inside the training studies alone.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The baselines are calibrated and the pipelines reproduce their sources}

If the stored quantiles describe healthy adults, then healthy adults a baseline has never seen should fall outside its range about 5\% of the time. This is what is observed. Across held-out cohorts and left-out baseline studies the median is 5.3\% of families in the assembly baseline and 4.0\% in the read baseline, and the picture for genes and pathways is the same (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}c). They are also reproducible outside the group that built them, since re-running either pipeline from deposited raw reads returns the percentiles of the resource it reproduces, with a median Spearman $\rho$ between 0.86 and 0.99 across layers in 61 MGnify samples and of 1.00 in 34 cMD3 samples (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}d). What the percentiles are worth as evidence can then be asked.

\subsection{Percentiles carry as much disease information as a published index}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{In studies left out of training, checkGM output separates cases from controls at least as well as published health indices.} Violet, checkGM output; 0.5 is chance. $\dagger$GMWI2 was trained on samples from 10 of these 14 studies, so the right-hand panel is its fair comparison.}
\label{fig:benchmark}
\end{figure*}

Because a percentile is a transformation of the profile rather than an addition to it, the first possibility to rule out is a loss of predictive power, and none is found (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}). Across 11 assembly-pipeline studies, each left out in turn, a mean AUROC of 0.62 is reached by the percentiles against 0.58 by raw abundances (higher in 9 of 11 studies, $p = 0.021$), 0.51 by alpha diversity and 0.48 by a genus-level GMHI approximation. The ordering is the same over 14 read-pipeline studies, where 0.69 is reached against 0.66 for raw abundances (10 of 14, $p = 0.039$) and 0.62 for the published GMHI (11 of 14, $p = 0.008$). Nor does the result depend on the inclusion tier: repeated against the strict assembly baseline, the benchmark gives 0.615 for the percentiles against 0.581 for raw abundances (7 of 11 studies, $p = 0.051$). Comparable information is carried by the gene families and pathways alone (0.67), but nothing is added on top of the taxa (0.69 either way), and refitting the supervised score with them changes nothing (0.73 against 0.72); the taxonomic percentiles are evidently doing the work.

The harder comparison is with GMWI2, which reaches 0.72 on the same 14 studies against 0.75 for the health score (higher in 10 of 14, $p = 0.22$). Those numbers should not be set against each other directly, and the reason is more informative than the gap between them. Matching sequencing-run accessions and ENA projects shows that GMWI2's training set contains samples from 10 of these 14 studies, whereas the score reported here was pre-specified and every study was scored by a model trained without it. The only clean comparison is therefore the four studies neither model has seen, on which the two are level at 0.62 against 0.61. Two classifiers cannot be separated by four studies and no such separation is claimed; the claim is narrower, that the accuracy of a state-of-the-art index is reached by a score whose every feature can be inspected.

Two cautions attach to that score, and neither is found to account for its accuracy. Processing artifacts are inherited by its species-presence features. \textit{[Collinsella] massiliensis} is present in most controls and absent from every case of three cMD3 studies, and this batch difference is seized upon by the model as its strongest single feature; it is nevertheless invisible to a percentile-only variant, which performs as well on the four unseen studies (0.64). When training is further restricted to the studies that contain both cases and controls, so that study identity cannot stand in for the label, 0.72 is still obtained.

\subsection{The same output explains itself, one patient at a time}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig4.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Reference ranges reproduce known disease associations, in one cohort and across nine.} (a) \cdiff{} infection: for the twelve families that separate cases from controls most, the share of the 56 patients in whom each is below the healthy range, expected but missing, or above it; diamonds, the same share among the study's 14 controls. (b) Colorectal cancer: shift in median percentile between cases and the controls of the same cohort, for the families, genera, MetaCyc pathways and KEGG orthologs that shift most consistently; dots mark significant shifts. Because every row is a percentile of the same baseline, $-30$ points means the same thing in every column and on every layer.}
\label{fig:known}
\end{figure*}

Since the two tie on prediction, whatever distinguishes them must lie in what their output means: a sample is reported by one to look diseased, while what is unusual within it is named by the other. In the \cdiff{} infection cohort of the assembly pipeline (56 patients, 14 controls) the obligate anaerobes are seen to drop out one family at a time. \textit{Oscillospiraceae} lies below the healthy range in 54\% of patients and in none of the controls, \textit{Atopobiaceae} is expected but missing in 59\%, and six further anaerobe families follow; in the other direction, \textit{Lactobacillaceae}, \textit{Enterococcaceae} and \textit{Streptococcaceae} rise above the range in roughly one patient in eight and, again, in no control (Fig.~\ref{fig:known}a). The loss of anaerobic colonization resistance that defines this infection is thus recovered without the controls being used at all, and the same report is produced for a patient who has no control group whatever (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c): a single line of it makes the case for the scale, since \textit{Enterobacteriaceae} is found 233$\times$ above the typical healthy adult yet inside the healthy range, at the 87th percentile --- a distinction no fold-change against a cohort mean could draw.

What holds within one cohort holds across many. The depletion of butyrate-associated taxa reported by meta-analyses of the same studies~\cite{thomas2019,wirbel2019} is recovered in the nine colorectal cancer cohorts of the read pipeline, \textit{Lachnospiraceae} being lower in cases in four (median $-35$ points), \textit{Oscillospiraceae} ($-33$), \textit{Bifidobacteriaceae} ($-29$), \textit{Lachnospira} and \textit{Fusicatenibacter} ($-25$) in three, and eight further genera in two (Fig.~\ref{fig:known}b). Because the unit is a percentile of one baseline, those numbers may be read across the columns of the panel; and because gene families and pathways share that same scale, they may be read down it as well. Consistent shifts are found in 100 MetaCyc pathways and 80 KEGG orthologs, 85 and 70 of them downward, with thiamin formation and the S-adenosyl-L-methionine cycle among the strongest.

\subsection{A healthy population the baseline does not fit}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Hadza hunter-gatherers sit outside an industrialized healthy range, and the reference ranges name the genera responsible.} All samples are healthy adults, each scored against a baseline rebuilt without its own study. (a) Share of features outside the healthy range per sample. (b) The common gut genera and gene families that drive it, with the same study's US participants and the rest of the baseline for comparison.}
\label{fig:population}
\end{figure*}

A reference range is a claim about a population, and the honest test of such a claim is a healthy population from which it was not built. Both baselines are dominated by European, North American and East Asian cohorts, and one constituent study is conspicuous in the calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}c): the Hadza hunter-gatherers of Tanzania, sequenced alongside Nepali and Californian comparison groups~\cite{carter2023}. Scored out of sample, a median of 20.6\% of families is placed outside the healthy range for Hadza samples against 4.4\% for other healthy adults, whereas the 12 US participants of that same study, processed identically, sit at 11.5\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:population}a); the excess is therefore a property of the population, not of the sequencing.

The genera responsible are named as well, which no single score could do (Fig.~\ref{fig:population}b): \textit{Treponema} is placed above the range in 71\% of Hadza samples, \textit{Sarcina} in 47\%, \textit{Anaerovibrio} in 42\% and \textit{Brachyspira} in 39\%, while \textit{Parasutterella}, \textit{Rikenella} and \textit{Sanguibacteroides} are low or missing in between a quarter and a third of them. The gene families move with the taxa: aspartate kinase falls below the range in 62\% of samples and lysozyme rises above it in 33\%. These are the industrialization-depleted taxa for which the Hadza microbiome is known~\cite{carter2023}, recovered as deviations from an industrialized range rather than from a matched control group. The result is a caution and a method at once, for outside the populations a baseline was built from the fraction outside is not a measure of ill health, and it is the per-feature output that tells the two apart.

\section{Discussion}

A single gut metagenome is given, by checkGM, a position among healthy adults processed the same way, and that position is shipped with the software producing it. Treated as a predictor, a score built on this output matches a published health index without inheriting that index's overlap with the test studies; treated as a report, it states which taxa, genes and pathways are unusual and by how much. One sample can accordingly be read without a control group, all samples are placed on one scale, and the baseline travels as statistics alone.

The limits are visible in the same figures. On the four studies neither model had seen, the score only ties GMWI2, and the recovery of known biology is in any case partial. \textit{Blautia} and \textit{Faecalibacterium} are found higher in cases in two inflammatory bowel disease studies, against their well-replicated depletion~\cite{lloydprice2019,franzosa2019}. Taxa that are enriched in colorectal cancer but rare in healthy adults, among them \textit{Fusobacterium nucleatum} and \textit{Parvimonas}, fall below the prevalence filter and receive no percentile at all, since a reference range can speak only about the features healthy people carry. The Hadza result bounds the claim geographically, and population cannot in either cohort be separated from study.

checkGM is a Python package under the MIT license; the repository holds the code, container recipes for both pipelines and the documentation, and the lenient baselines (assembly v0.8, read v0.2) and the container image are deposited on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23181418), the strict tier being rebuilt from the archived configuration by the same script. Reference intervals became useful in clinical chemistry once they were defined per assay, published with the method, and re-derived for the populations actually being tested. checkGM is an attempt to meet those three requirements for gut metagenomes.
